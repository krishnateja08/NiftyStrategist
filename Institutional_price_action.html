Why price drops before it rallies

Thirteen reasons a chart moves the way it does before the "real" move starts. Pick a tab, expand a pattern, then test yourself in the Quiz tab.

Four ways a stock dips right before it actually rallies — some engineered, some just noise clearing out.

01 / STOP-LOSS HUNTING

The Shakeout

Retail stop-losses cluster just below obvious support — a round number, yesterday's low, a visible trendline. A sharp, fast poke below that level triggers all of them at once, forcing weak-handed sellers out at the worst possible price.

The same move that stops out retail supplies the exact liquidity a larger buyer needs to fill a big order without pushing price up first.

Spot it: a quick spike below support on a single candle, followed by an immediate, sharp reclaim — not a slow grind down.
SUPPORT SWEPT → IMMEDIATE RECLAIM
02 / WYCKOFF ACCUMULATION

The Spring

After a stock trades sideways in a range for a while, smart money often pushes price briefly below the range low — a "spring" — to test whether any real supply remains. If sellers don't show up in size, the range is exhausted and markup can begin.

This differs from a random shakeout because it happens after a visible base, not out of nowhere — the sideways range is the tell.

Spot it: weeks of sideways chop, then one dip below the range on lower volume than the range itself, followed by a strong push back above the range.
RANGE → SPRING BELOW LOW → MARKUP
03 / PANIC & MEAN REVERSION

The Overreaction

Not every dip is engineered. News, a broad market wobble, or index-linked selling can push a stock down sharply with no one "hunting" anything — it's just short-term fear outrunning the fundamentals.

Once the panic sellers are done, price often snaps back toward where it was, simply because nothing about the business actually changed.

Spot it: a V-shaped drop and recovery tied to a news headline or broad index move, with no prior range or support level involved.
SHARP V-DROP → FAST MEAN REVERSION
04 / LIQUIDITY MECHANICS

The Iceberg Push

A large buyer can't fill their whole order at the current price without moving the market against themselves. So they sell small size into the bids first, nudging price down slightly, then absorb everything offered at that lower price before marking it back up.

It's less a "trap" and more just how a big position gets built quietly — a few small red candles doing the work of a discount.

Spot it: a shallow, gradual dip on unremarkable volume, no clear news trigger, followed by steady absorption and a slow grind higher.
SHALLOW DIP → QUIET ABSORPTION → GRIND UP

Related structures and mechanics — distribution, gaps, VWAP, sessions, options positioning, and momentum divergence.

05 / DISTRIBUTION AT HIGHS

The Upthrust

The mirror image of a spring, but at resistance instead of support. Price spikes above an obvious high, triggering breakout buyers and forcing short-sellers to cover — then reverses hard back into the range instead of continuing.

This is Wyckoff's distribution-phase pattern: smart money using the breakout excitement to sell into strength before marking price back down.

Spot it: a sharp poke above a well-tested resistance level on a breakout-volume candle, followed by an immediate reversal back inside the range — the inverse of the spring.
RANGE → SPIKE ABOVE HIGH → REVERSAL
06 / IMBALANCE

Fair Value Gap Fill

A fast, one-directional move — a gap-up on news, for example — leaves a price zone where almost no real two-sided trading happened. That zone is an "imbalance."

Price frequently returns to fill that gap later, not because anyone is manipulating it, but because that zone has no real trading history yet and acts like a magnet before the original move continues.

Spot it: a candle (or gap) that leaves a visible empty space on the chart between two candle wicks, followed later by a pullback that trades right through that empty zone.
GAP UP → LATER PULLBACK FILLS GAP
07 / EXECUTION BENCHMARK

VWAP Reversion

A lot of institutional execution algorithms are benchmarked against VWAP (volume-weighted average price) — their job is to trade at or better than it, not to chase extremes.

When price stretches far from VWAP intraday, especially in a range-bound session, that stretch tends to pull back toward VWAP simply because so much of the day's volume is anchored to trading around it.

Spot it: price runs well away from the VWAP line intraday without strong trend conviction, then drifts back toward it later in the session.
STRETCH FROM VWAP → DRIFT BACK
08 / OPENING RANGE

The ORB Fakeout

The first 15–30 minutes of a session often gets tested and rejected before the real move of the day. Price breaks the opening range high or low, draws in breakout entries, then fails and reverses through the range.

This is exactly what "chase guards" in an ORB engine are built to filter — entering on the first break without confirmation means entering right into this fakeout.

Spot it: a break of the opening 15–30 minute high/low that reverses back inside the range within a few candles, before the real directional move develops later.
OR BREAK → FAILS → REAL MOVE OPPOSITE
09 / OPTIONS POSITIONING

OI Wall Magnetism

Near expiry, price tends to gravitate toward strikes carrying the heaviest open interest. Option writers hedge their exposure to minimize total payout, and that hedging activity itself pushes price toward those "max pain" strikes.

This isn't a chart shape so much as a price-magnet effect — the OI walls you already compute in your Nifty/BankNifty report are the direct output of this mechanism.

Spot it: price chop that repeatedly stalls at, or drifts toward, one specific strike as expiry approaches, more than pure price action would explain.
CHOP CONVERGING TOWARD OI-HEAVY STRIKE
10 / EXHAUSTION SIGNAL

Divergence

Price makes a new high, but momentum (RSI) or volume doesn't confirm it — each fresh high comes with weaker underlying strength than the last. That's a divergence, and it's often the early warning sign that precedes one of the reversal patterns above.

Divergence isn't a trigger by itself — it just flags that a move is running out of fuel, which is exactly when a spring, upthrust, or shakeout tends to show up next.

Spot it: price prints a higher high while RSI or volume prints a lower high alongside it — the two lines disagreeing is the signal.
PRICE HIGHER HIGH · RSI LOWER HIGH

Every pattern above can look identical to a real breakdown while it's happening. Volume and order flow are the filter that separates the two — this is where "the shape looks right" becomes "the shape is confirmed."

11 / DELTA CONFIRMATION

Volume Delta & CVD

Volume delta is buy volume minus sell volume on each candle. Cumulative Volume Delta (CVD) adds that up over time, giving you a running read on whether buyers or sellers are actually in control — independent of what price itself is doing.

Absorption is the tell: price drops toward support, but CVD keeps rising. That divergence means aggressive sellers are hitting the bid, but a large passive buyer is soaking up every share at that price without letting it break — the exact mechanism behind a spring or a shakeout.

Spot it: price makes a new low into a support zone while the CVD line makes a higher low alongside it — price and delta disagreeing is the signal.
PRICE LOWER LOW · CVD HIGHER LOW = ABSORPTION
12 / VOLUME PROFILE

POC, VAH & VAL

Volume profile plots how much volume traded at each price level rather than over time. The Point of Control (POC) is the single price with the most volume; the Value Area High and Low (VAH/VAL) bracket the range where roughly 70% of volume traded.

High-Volume Nodes (HVN) mark prices the market spent a long time agreeing on — they tend to act as support/resistance because so many participants have a position there. Low-Volume Nodes (LVN) mark prices the market rejected quickly, so price tends to move through them fast when revisited.

Spot it: price stalls and chops at an HVN but slices straight through an LVN with almost no hesitation — the profile shape predicts the speed of the move before it happens.
HORIZONTAL VOLUME BY PRICE, NOT TIME
13 / ORDER MECHANICS

Aggressive vs. Passive Orders

Passive orders (limit orders) sit in the book and provide liquidity. Aggressive orders (market orders) cross the spread and consume that liquidity. Every fakeout ultimately comes down to this mechanism: an aggressive push through a level either finds enough passive supply to hold it back, or it doesn't.

A spring or shakeout is aggressive selling running into a wall of passive buying that doesn't budge. A real breakdown is aggressive selling finding thin passive support that gives way — same-looking candle, opposite order-book reality underneath it.

Spot it: on a level break, does price stall and print small-bodied candles right at the level (passive orders absorbing), or does it slice through with large, low-resistance candles (passive orders thin or absent)?
MARKET ORDERS CONSUME LIMIT-ORDER LIQUIDITY
